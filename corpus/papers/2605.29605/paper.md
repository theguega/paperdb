###### Abstract

Confidence estimation for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models is essential for robots to perform manipulation tasks in the open world, providing crucial signals for risk-sensitive decision-making and failure anticipation. Existing confidence estimation methods typically rely on ensemble-based paradigms or action-token probabilities to predict the likelihood of task success. However, they still encounter challenges in computational efficiency and cross-architecture generalizability. These methods usually require repeated sampling, leading to inference inefficiency, and are restricted to VLA models with discrete action outputs, making them difficult to apply to continuous action spaces. To address this issue, we propose VLAConf, a one-class discriminative confidence framework. By leveraging frozen pretrained VLA internal representations, VLAConf directly estimates step-wise anomaly scores in a single forward pass using a lightweight confidence head, thereby eliminating the overhead of exhaustive resampling. We additionally use step-conditioned modeling to encode rollout-phase information along the manipulation trajectory. Experiments on the LIBERO benchmark demonstrate that VLAConf significantly improves the quality of the confidence signal constructed for post-hoc calibration, outperforming existing baselines by a large margin in inference efficiency. The effectiveness of VLAConf is further validated in real-robot experiments. To access the source code and supplementary videos, visit https://sites.google.com/view/vlaconf.

## I INTRODUCTION

Confidence estimation is essential for deploying Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in open-world robotic manipulation, as illustrated in Figure[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ I INTRODUCTION ‣ VLAConf: Calibrated Task-Success Confidence for Vision-Language-Action Models"). Although VLA models have shown strong cross-task generalization, real-world execution still involves distribution shifts, long-tail scenarios, and unexpected failures. Therefore, a robot should not only generate control actions, but also predict how likely it is to successfully complete the current task. Such confidence supports risk-sensitive decision-making, failure anticipation, action rollback, and human-robot collaboration. Motivated by the need for a reliable estimate of task success itself, we study how to derive a success-relevant signal from pretrained VLA representations and calibrate it into task-success confidence for reliable evaluation.

![Image 1: Refer to caption](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605v1/images/intro_v1.png)

Figure 1: Motivation of VLAConf. Despite strong generalization, VLA policies can still encounter shifted scenes, distribution shifts, and execution errors in open-world manipulation, highlighting the need for calibrated task-success confidence to distinguish low-confidence situations from high-confidence executions.

Existing VLA confidence methods predominantly follow an ensemble-based paradigm, where confidence is derived from predictive disagreement across multiple models or prompts and then calibrated post hoc. Although intuitive, this paradigm is difficult to deploy in modern VLA systems: repeated sampling introduces substantial computational overhead under real-time control constraints, and disagreement over continuous action outputs can be unstable and difficult to interpret as task-level success likelihood. A complementary line of uncertainty estimation and failure detection research in robot manipulation has explored one-class and distance-based formulations, which can avoid repeated sampling and provide direct scores for continuous-control contexts. However, these methods have largely been studied in task-specific visuomotor policies, leaving it unclear how to extend them to generalist VLAs with heterogeneous visual and linguistic inputs.

To address this challenge, we propose VLAConf, a one-class discriminative confidence framework that leverages frozen pretrained VLA representations to construct an efficient confidence signal for robotic manipulation. Instead of using repeated sampling and post hoc disagreement aggregation, VLAConf estimates confidence in a single forward pass using a lightweight confidence head built on top of the pretrained VLA backbone. Concretely, it pools visual and language hidden states from the VLA, incorporates proprioceptive state to encode the current execution context, and trains a Coin-Flip Network objective to learn a one-class scoring function over the resulting representation space, such that typical training-distribution contexts are associated with low-norm coin vectors while atypical contexts yield larger scores. To account for the temporal structure of manipulation, the scoring head is step-conditioned so that the same execution context can be interpreted relative to rollout phase. The resulting confidence signal is efficient, transferable, and backbone-agnostic, and can be calibrated into task-success confidence for reliable VLA deployment.

## II Related Work

### II-A Confidence Estimation and Uncertainty in VLAs

Recent vision-language-action (VLA) models have demonstrated strong semantic grounding and broad manipulation capabilities by combining pretrained vision-language backbones with action generation modules [[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib1), [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib2), [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib3), [4](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib4), [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib5)]. Despite this progress, evaluating the reliability of VLA systems during deployment remains challenging. In the broader context of large language and vision-language models, uncertainty quantification often relies on token-level probabilities, semantic entropy, or generation consistency [[6](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib6), [7](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib7), [8](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib8), [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib9), [10](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib10)].

Inspired by these approaches, initial efforts toward confidence estimation for VLAs have predominantly followed an ensemble-based paradigm [[11](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib11), [12](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib12), [13](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib13)]. Notably, ConfidenceVLA [[14](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib14)] shares our goal of providing calibrated task-success confidence for VLA models. They achieve this by deriving confidence through predictive disagreement across prompt ensembles and action-token probabilities. While conceptually appealing, this strategy faces two critical limitations in modern robotic deployment. First, repeated sampling and ensemble inference are computationally expensive, making them difficult to reconcile with the high-frequency, real-time control requirements of physical robots. Second, relying on action-token probabilities restricts these methods to discrete-action VLAs. For state-of-the-art VLAs that output continuous actions (e.g., via diffusion or flow-matching [[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib1)]), disagreement-based uncertainty is often unstable and difficult to interpret directly as a success likelihood. To achieve an efficient, continuous-action-compatible confidence signal, we must shift our focus from the action-output space to the model’s internal representation space.

### II-B Out-of-Distribution and Failure Detection in Robotics

Extracting reliability signals from internal representations is deeply connected to out-of-distribution (OOD) and failure detection in robot manipulation. A practical regime assumes that failures are scarce, expensive, or unsafe to collect, meaning successful executions provide the only reliable supervision. Traditional OOD methods—such as density estimation, one-class discriminators, distance metrics, and consistency checks [[15](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib15), [16](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib16), [17](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib17), [18](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib18), [19](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib19), [20](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib20), [21](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib21)]—excel in this regime because they are strictly _success-only_. Recent works, such as FAIL-Detect [[16](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib16)], systematically evaluate these methods for generative imitation learning policies. We share this success-only philosophy, but these methods have largely been designed for task-specific visuomotor policies. Scaling them to generalist VLAs introduces a significant multi-task generalization challenge, as the diverse visual scenes and novel language instructions inherent to generalist deployment can easily be misclassified as OOD anomalies.

To handle the complexity of generalist policies, a separate line of work explores supervised failure detection [[22](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib22), [23](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib23), [24](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib24), [25](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib25)]. For instance, SAFE [[26](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib26)] demonstrates that the internal latent features of VLAs capture rich, task-agnostic semantics about execution success, and successfully trains a multi-task failure classifier on top of these representations. While this resolves the multi-task generalization issue, it fundamentally relies on collecting diverse _failed_ rollouts for supervised training, which violates the practical success-only constraint and poses significant safety risks in real-world open-vocabulary manipulation.

Positioning of Our Approach. Our work, VLAConf, synthesizes these perspectives to provide a practical confidence signal for modern VLAs. Like ConfidenceVLA [[14](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib14)], our primary objective is to produce calibrated task-success confidence. However, we achieve this in a single forward pass without relying on discrete action tokens or expensive ensembles. To do so, we adopt the _success-only_ philosophy of traditional OOD methods [[16](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib16)] by formulating confidence estimation as a one-class discriminative problem. However, we apply it directly to the frozen, rich latent representations of VLAs—similar to supervised methods like SAFE [[26](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib26)]—to ensure multi-task generalization. By training a lightweight confidence head with a Coin-Flip Network objective on successful rollouts alone, VLAConf bridges these paradigms, delivering an efficient, backbone-agnostic confidence measure naturally suited for continuous-action generalist policies.

## III Problem Definition

We study task-success confidence estimation for deployed vision-language-action (VLA) policies in language-conditioned robotic manipulation, as shown in the upper part of Figure[2](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ III Problem Definition ‣ VLAConf: Calibrated Task-Success Confidence for Vision-Language-Action Models"). Given a language instruction l and an observed execution prefix \rho_{t}=(o_{1},\dots,o_{t}), where o_{t}=(I_{t},x_{t}) contains the current image and proprioceptive state, the goal is to estimate the probability that the current rollout will eventually succeed under the deployed policy, p(Y=1\mid\rho_{t},l), where Y\in\{0,1\} denotes the final rollout-level task outcome. This formulation covers both pre-execution confidence, where only the initial observation is available, and online execution confidence, where the estimate is updated as the rollout unfolds. The latter is important for manipulation because task progress and emerging failures are often only visible after execution begins.

We assume a practical imitation-learning workflow. The VLA policy is first pretrained on broad robot data and then adapted to the target task using successful demonstrations, denoted by \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sft}}. For confidence learning, we use the observation-language traces of these demonstrations, written as \tau_{i}=(o_{i,1:T_{i}},l_{i}), where l_{i} is the language instruction and o_{i,k}=(I_{i,k},x_{i,k}). Action labels used for policy adaptation are omitted from the notation because VLAConf keeps the policy parameters fixed and learns confidence on top of frozen VLA representations. These demonstrations are not collected for confidence learning and contain no failed executions.

![Image 2: Refer to caption](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605v1/images/problem_formulation.png)

Figure 2: Problem formulation for VLA confidence estimation. Top: given an instruction and an observed rollout prefix, the goal is to estimate the probability that the current execution will eventually succeed. Bottom: VLAConf decomposes this problem into step-wise score construction followed by prefix aggregation and calibration.

In addition, a small set of completed policy rollouts can be labeled with binary task outcomes; we denote this outcome-labeled set by \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}=\{(\rho^{(j)},l^{(j)},Y^{(j)})\}_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cal}}}. VLAConf uses the two sources separately: \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sft}} defines the support of normal successful execution and is used to learn an uncalibrated anomaly signal, while \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}} is used only to calibrate that scalar signal into a task-success probability. Thus, representation-level confidence learning remains success-only, and scarce outcome labels are reserved for low-capacity probability calibration.

As summarized in the lower part of Figure[2](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ III Problem Definition ‣ VLAConf: Calibrated Task-Success Confidence for Vision-Language-Action Models"), VLAConf first produces step-wise anomaly scores and aggregates the observed prefix into a scalar signal u_{t}. It then fits a calibration map h(\cdot), implemented as Platt scaling in our experiments, to convert this signal into p(Y=1\mid\rho_{t},l)=h(u_{t}).

## IV VLAConf

### IV-A Overview

An overview of VLAConf is shown in Figure LABEL:fig:vlaconf_overview. Given a rollout prefix \rho_{t}=(o_{1},\dots,o_{t}) and instruction l, VLAConf estimates task-success confidence by measuring whether the observed execution remains close to the success manifold represented by \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sft}}. The method first extracts a step-wise execution representation from a frozen VLA, maps each step to an _anomaly score_—following standard one-class OOD detection terminology, a non-negative scalar that grows when the input falls outside the support of successful executions—with a step-conditioned scoring head, and finally aggregates and calibrates these scores into p(Y=1\mid\rho_{t},l).

We first describe how VLAConf constructs backbone-agnostic execution features from frozen VLA hidden states. We then introduce the step-conditioned anomaly scoring objective trained only on successful trajectories. Finally, we present prefix-level aggregation and post-hoc calibration, which convert step-wise anomaly scores into calibrated task-success probabilities.

### IV-B Frozen VLA Execution Representation

The first step is to obtain a representation on which support can be estimated consistently across VLA architectures. We estimate confidence in the _internal representation space_ of a pretrained VLA rather than in its action-output space. This is important because manipulation failures are frequently reflected by whether the current multimodal context departs from familiar successful behavior, not merely by whether the immediate action prediction appears sharp. Moreover, action probabilities are often unavailable or hard to compare across architectures, especially when the action head is continuous rather than token based.

Let f_{\theta} denote a pretrained VLA backbone. We keep f_{\theta} frozen throughout confidence learning. This choice preserves the semantic and geometric priors already learned by the VLA and keeps confidence estimation efficient because only a small head is optimized. Thus, VLAConf treats the pretrained VLA as a general-purpose multimodal feature extractor and learns confidence on top of that representation rather than by adapting the policy itself.

At step k, the backbone processes the current observation-instruction pair (o_{k},l) and returns hidden states \mathcal{H}_{k}. Although different backbones expose these hidden states through different tensor layouts, VLAConf only assumes a shared pooled-feature interface. We separately pool the valid visual tokens and language tokens, yielding a visual summary h_{k}^{v} and a language summary h_{k}^{l}. These summaries capture the scene content and task semantics needed for confidence estimation. We additionally fuse the robot proprioceptive state because visual-language context alone may not fully determine whether the current execution phase is healthy. Variables such as gripper opening and end-effector pose often distinguish between a normal state and an abnormal one even when the image appears similar. We project the proprioceptive component x_{k} with a small state encoder and fuse it with the pooled visual-language features:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{k}\displaystyle=f_{\theta}(o_{k},l),
\displaystyle h_{k}^{v}\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pool}_{v}(\mathcal{H}_{k}),\qquad h_{k}^{l}=\mathrm{Pool}_{l}(\mathcal{H}_{k}),
\displaystyle h_{k}^{x}\displaystyle=E_{x}(x_{k}),\qquad z_{k}=M_{\text{mix}}([h_{k}^{v};h_{k}^{l};h_{k}^{x}]),

where \mathrm{Pool}_{v}(\cdot) and \mathrm{Pool}_{l}(\cdot) denote masked mean pooling over the valid visual and language token positions, E_{x}(\cdot) is a small MLP state encoder, and M_{\text{mix}}(\cdot) is a two-layer mixing MLP. The result is a compact execution representation z_{k} on which confidence is learned. This representation hides backbone-specific implementation details while retaining the visual, semantic, and proprioceptive information needed to assess familiarity.

### IV-C Step-Conditioned Anomaly Scoring

Given the execution feature z_{k}, we next learn a scalar anomaly score without requiring failure labels. We build this score on the Coin-Flip Network (CFN) of Lobel et al.[[27](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib27)], which fits a small head q(\cdot) to predict an i.i.d. Rademacher target c\sim\{-1,+1\}^{d} for every input. Under the squared-error optimum, the network output for an input z encountered N(z) times collapses to the empirical mean of its targets, meaning its expected squared norm is proportional to 1/N(z). The squared norm is therefore a smooth anomaly score: features common in the training distribution yield small norms, while atypical features yield large ones. This makes CFN a natural fit for our success-only setting.

Importantly, manipulation confidence is inherently phase-dependent: the same scene may correspond to normal behavior at one stage of a task and abnormal behavior at another (e.g., an open gripper is expected before grasp but suspicious after lift). Therefore, we condition the anomaly scoring head on the current rollout step, allowing the model to learn phase-specific notions of familiarity.

To encode task phase, we introduce a step encoder that maps the rollout step index k to a compact descriptor. Let K be a fixed normalization horizon and let \hat{k}=\mathrm{clip}(k{-}1,0,K{-}1). The descriptor combines a learned step embedding with a normalized progress scalar:

\psi_{k}=[\,e_{k};\,\bar{k}\,],\qquad e_{k}=\mathrm{Emb}(\hat{k}),\qquad\bar{k}=\hat{k}/(K{-}1),

where \mathrm{Emb}(\cdot) is a learned embedding table of size K. Using a fixed K rather than the per-trajectory length T_{i} keeps \bar{k} well-defined in the online setting, where the eventual rollout length is unknown; the clip ensures both e_{k} and \bar{k} stay in range when k exceeds K{-}1 at inference. The resulting descriptor captures both discrete phase identity and coarse relative progress through the rollout.

Instead of concatenating \psi_{k} with z_{k}, we use the descriptor to _modulate_ the anomaly scoring head with a FiLM-style affine transform[[28](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib28)] followed by a residual gate, allowing the same execution representation to be interpreted differently at different phases of the task. Let W_{\text{proj}}(\cdot) denote a \mathrm{Linear}{\to}\mathrm{LN}{\to}\mathrm{Tanh} pre-projection, A_{\text{cond}}(\cdot) a two-layer MLP that encodes \psi_{k}, and q(\cdot) a two-layer MLP coin-value head. We write

\displaystyle h_{k}\displaystyle=W_{\text{proj}}(z_{k}),\qquad c_{k}=A_{\text{cond}}(\psi_{k}),
\displaystyle(\gamma_{k},\beta_{k})\displaystyle=W_{\text{film}}(c_{k}),\qquad\eta_{k}=\sigma\!\big(W_{\text{gate}}(c_{k})\big),
\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{k}\displaystyle=\mathrm{LN}\!\big((1+\gamma_{k})\odot h_{k}+\beta_{k}\big),
\displaystyle\tilde{z}_{k}\displaystyle=\eta_{k}\odot\tilde{h}_{k}+(1-\eta_{k})\odot h_{k},
\displaystyle v_{k}\displaystyle=q(\tilde{z}_{k}),\qquad s_{k}=\|v_{k}\|_{2}^{2},

where \sigma(\cdot) is the sigmoid, \odot denotes element-wise multiplication, W_{\text{film}} and W_{\text{gate}} are independent linear maps that share the upstream conditioning network A_{\text{cond}}, and the post-affine \mathrm{LN} stabilizes the modulated branch before gating. The modulated feature \tilde{z}_{k} is then mapped to a vector v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}, whose squared norm defines the step-wise anomaly score s_{k}.

We train the head with a success-only objective over the step-wise samples extracted from successful rollouts:

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{step}}^{+}=\{(o_{i,k},l_{i},k):\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sft}},\;k=1,\dots,T_{i}\}.

For each sample, the head predicts a vector v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}. Instead of using success labels, each sample is assigned a deterministic pseudo-random target c_{i,k}=\Gamma(i,k)\in\{-1,+1\}^{d}, where \Gamma is a deterministic generator. The head is then optimized by mean squared error:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFN}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{step}}^{+}|}\sum_{\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sft}}}\sum_{k=1}^{T_{i}}\|v_{i,k}-c_{i,k}\|_{2}^{2}.

At inference time, the squared norm \|v_{k}\|_{2}^{2} is directly used as the step-wise anomaly score s_{k}, which preserves a useful ordering of in- vs. out-of-distribution evidence.

### IV-D Prefix Aggregation and Calibration

The step-wise anomaly score s_{k} is local and uncalibrated, whereas the target quantity is rollout-level success probability. A robot may briefly visit an unusual state and still recover, or it may appear normal early in the rollout and fail only after a later deviation. Confidence should therefore summarize the evidence accumulated across the observed prefix rather than depend on a single step in isolation.

We therefore define a trajectory-level aggregated signal u_{t}=\phi(s_{1},\dots,s_{t}), where \phi(\cdot) aggregates the step-wise scores up to time t. The most suitable aggregation rule depends on the deployment protocol. In the pre-execution setting, only the initial observation is available, so we set u_{1}=s_{1}. In the online setting, we consider simple aggregation rules such as the running mean, u_{t}=\tfrac{1}{t}\sum_{k=1}^{t}s_{k}, or the maximum prefix score. This modularity allows the same step-wise model to support both one-shot risk assessment before execution and continuous confidence tracking during rollout.

The aggregated signal u_{t} is an uncalibrated scalar. To convert it into an interpretable success probability, we perform post-hoc calibration on a small set of outcome-labeled policy rollouts, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}} (as introduced in Section[III](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#S3 "III Problem Definition ‣ VLAConf: Calibrated Task-Success Confidence for Vision-Language-Action Models")). This is the only stage of VLAConf that consumes outcome labels, requiring only a binary outcome Y\in\{0,1\} per rollout rather than dense per-step annotations.

We fit a standard Platt scaling model[[29](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib29)] to map the aggregated signal to a success probability:

h(u_{t})=\operatorname{sigmoid}(-\alpha\,u_{t}+\beta),

where \alpha\geq 0 and \beta\in\mathbb{R} are learned by minimizing the binary cross-entropy on \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}. The explicit minus sign encodes the prior that larger anomaly scores indicate lower probability of success.

Overall, the pipeline adds only one MLP head and one scalar logistic regression per step, with negligible overhead relative to the VLA backbone and no additional sampling.

## V Experiments

### V-A Experimental Setup

Backbones. We evaluate VLAConf on two representative VLA backbones spanning the discrete- and continuous-action regimes: (i) OpenVLA-OFT[[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib1)], a token-based VLA that produces actions through a discrete autoregressive head; and (ii) \pi^{0.5}[[2](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib2)], a state-of-the-art generalist VLA that emits continuous actions via flow-matching. Both backbones are kept frozen during confidence learning. This pair lets us examine whether the same confidence signal can be constructed across heterogeneous action heads, and is also the setting under which prior action-token-based confidence methods break down.

Benchmarks. We use the LIBERO benchmark suite[[30](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib30)] for in-distribution evaluation, covering three subsets that stress different aspects of manipulation: LIBERO-Goal, LIBERO-Spatial, and LIBERO-Object. For robustness under shifted conditions, we additionally evaluate on LIBERO-Pro[[31](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib31)] and LIBERO-Plus[[32](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib32)], which extend LIBERO with perturbations and previously unseen tasks (Section[V-C](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#S5.SS3 "V-C Robustness under Shifted LIBERO Suites ‣ V Experiments ‣ VLAConf: Calibrated Task-Success Confidence for Vision-Language-Action Models")). Real-robot results are reported in Section[V-D](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#S5.SS4 "V-D Real-World Robot Experiments ‣ V Experiments ‣ VLAConf: Calibrated Task-Success Confidence for Vision-Language-Action Models").

Baselines. We compare against three external reference points and one ablation. (i) PCA-kmeans: a post-hoc clustering OOD baseline adapted from robot execution failure detection[[33](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib33)]. It projects frozen VLA features with PCA, clusters successful demonstration features with K-means, and uses the distance to the nearest centroid as an anomaly score. (ii) TokenProb: the policy’s own action-token probability, aggregated over the action sequence. This lower-cost reference re-uses information already produced by the VLA. (iii) ConfidenceVLA[[14](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib14)]: a prompt-ensemble confidence method that derives task-success probability from action-token agreement across resampled prompts. TokenProb and ConfidenceVLA rely on per-token action probabilities and are therefore restricted to VLAs with discrete action heads; they are not directly applicable to continuous-action backbones such as \pi^{0.5}, and the corresponding entries are marked “/” in Table[I](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#S4.T1 "TABLE I ‣ IV-D Prefix Aggregation and Calibration ‣ IV VLAConf ‣ VLAConf: Calibrated Task-Success Confidence for Vision-Language-Action Models"). (iv) VLAConf-NoStep: an ablation of VLAConf that directly transfers prior one-class discriminator ideas, such as RND and CFM-style success-only scoring[[34](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib34), [35](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib35)], to frozen VLA representations. It uses the same frozen VLA features and one-class scoring objective as VLAConf, but removes the step encoder, isolating the effect of phase conditioning.

Evaluation protocol. For each deployed policy, we reserve a small validation set of completed rollouts as \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}, used to fit the two Platt-scaling parameters. We then evaluate on disjoint held-out rollouts and record, per rollout, (a) the binary outcome Y\in\{0,1\}, (b) the rollout length, and (c) confidence queries from each method. Confidence is queried in two regimes: pre-execution, where only the initial observation o_{1} is available, and online execution, where the prefix \rho_{t} accumulates over the rollout and the available step-wise scores are aggregated (Section[IV](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#S4 "IV VLAConf ‣ VLAConf: Calibrated Task-Success Confidence for Vision-Language-Action Models")). For scalar online-execution table entries, we report the fixed checkpoint at 50\% of policy execution (using max aggregation), so the comparison reflects an in-progress estimate available during deployment.

Metrics. We report three calibration metrics that are standard for binary outcomes and capture complementary aspects of probability quality: Expected Calibration Error (ECE) measures the average gap between predicted confidence and empirical accuracy across confidence bins; Brier score measures the mean squared error between predicted probability and the binary outcome; and negative log-likelihood (NLL) penalizes overconfident wrong predictions more aggressively than Brier. Lower is better for all three. We additionally report task Success Rate of the underlying policy and the Average Inference Time per confidence query, measured on the same hardware.

Implementation details. The CFN head is a two-layer MLP applied on top of frozen VLA features, with masked-mean pooling over visual and language tokens and a small MLP encoder for proprioceptive state. The step descriptor consists of a learned embedding \mathrm{Emb}(\hat{k}) together with a normalized progress scalar \hat{k}/(K{-}1), where \hat{k}=\mathrm{clip}(k{-}1,0,K{-}1) and K is a fixed hyperparameter (K{=}96 for \pi^{0.5}, K{=}256 for OpenVLA-OFT). The descriptor modulates the coin-scoring head through a FiLM affine transform with a post-affine LayerNorm and a sigmoid residual gate. Coin-vector dimension is d=64. We train the head with AdamW for the same number of optimization steps across backbones and suites; the only stage that consumes outcome labels is the per-suite Platt scaling. Unless otherwise stated, temporal analyses use the running mean of step-wise scores, while the main scalar online results use max aggregation as specified above.

Evaluation organization. We organize experiments into standard LIBERO, shifted LIBERO-Pro/Plus, and real-robot evaluations.

(1) VLAConf improves probability quality while remaining efficient. On OpenVLA-OFT, where all baselines are applicable, VLAConf offers the best trade-off between calibration quality and inference cost. TokenProb is the cheapest baseline because it directly reuses the policy’s own action-token probabilities, but its averaged Brier score and NLL are worse than those of VLAConf. PCA-kmeans is also consistently weaker, indicating that a simple clustering distance in frozen feature space is not sufficient. ConfidenceVLA is much more expensive because it repeatedly queries the VLA under a prompt ensemble, taking 712.9 ms per confidence query on average. In contrast, VLAConf adds only a lightweight head on top of frozen policy features and runs at 64.9 ms, essentially matching TokenProb (65.1 ms) while being about 11\times faster than ConfidenceVLA. Despite this efficiency gap, VLAConf is strongest under the proper scoring rules in the averaged OpenVLA-OFT comparison. ConfidenceVLA still obtains the lowest online ECE, but this does not imply a more discriminative confidence signal: its prompt-ensemble score is concentrated and then Platt-scaled close to the empirical success rate, which can reduce bin-level calibration error while leaving little separation between successful and failed rollouts. The joint improvement on Brier score and NLL therefore provides stronger evidence that VLAConf produces sharper per-rollout probabilities rather than only matching the average success rate.

(2) The same confidence head applies across action parameterizations. VLAConf runs unchanged on both the discrete-action OpenVLA-OFT backbone and the continuous-action \pi^{0.5} backbone, while TokenProb and ConfidenceVLA are unavailable for \pi^{0.5} because they require discrete action-token probabilities. On \pi^{0.5}, VLAConf remains applicable on the LIBERO average, with pre-execution NLL of 0.3141 and online NLL of 0.2501. Compared with VLAConf-NoStep, it also gives lower Brier score and NLL in both regimes, showing that action-agnostic applicability alone is not enough; the representation-level score still benefits from phase conditioning. More broadly, because VLAConf only assumes access to frozen internal representations rather than a particular action parameterization, the same design can naturally extend to recent World Action Model policies that expose visual or dynamics features for control and planning[[36](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib36), [37](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#bib.bib37)].

(3) Online execution evidence is often helpful, but its benefit is not uniform. Comparing the pre-execution and online columns in Table[I](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#S4.T1 "TABLE I ‣ IV-D Prefix Aggregation and Calibration ‣ IV VLAConf ‣ VLAConf: Calibrated Task-Success Confidence for Vision-Language-Action Models"), VLAConf benefits from observing the rollout prefix on OpenVLA-OFT: at the fixed-50\% checkpoint, averaged Brier score and NLL both improve. The VLAConf-NoStep ablation further clarifies where this gain comes from. It keeps the same frozen features and success-only scoring objective, yet removing the step encoder weakens Brier score and NLL in most settings, especially in online OpenVLA-OFT results. This suggests that execution evidence must be interpreted relative to task progress rather than treated as an unordered set of familiar or unfamiliar states. Figure[3](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ V-A Experimental Setup ‣ V Experiments ‣ VLAConf: Calibrated Task-Success Confidence for Vision-Language-Action Models") shows a more nuanced temporal picture: some backbone–aggregator combinations improve substantially once task-relevant progress becomes visible, whereas others remain flat or degrade near the terminal stage. Thus, online evidence can sharpen confidence estimates, but the magnitude and timing of the benefit depend on both the backbone and the aggregation rule.

Together, these observations support our central claim: shifting confidence estimation from the action-output space to the frozen representation space yields sharper task-success probabilities at low inference cost, applies across discrete- and continuous-action VLAs, and can become more reliable as online execution evidence accumulates.

### V-C Robustness under Shifted LIBERO Suites

The standard LIBERO results establish the behavior of VLAConf under the original benchmark distribution. We next evaluate on LIBERO-Pro and LIBERO-Plus to test whether the confidence signal remains useful under systematic distribution shifts, including task, object, layout, camera, robot-state, lighting, background, and sensor perturbations. Following the evaluation protocol above, each shifted suite uses lightweight per-suite Platt calibration before held-out evaluation. To keep the main text focused, Table[II](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#S5.T2 "TABLE II ‣ V-B Main Results ‣ V Experiments ‣ VLAConf: Calibrated Task-Success Confidence for Vision-Language-Action Models") reports average online execution results across perturbation families; the full perturbation-level pre-execution and online results are provided in Appendix Tables[V](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#A3.T5 "TABLE V ‣ Appendix C Full Perturbation-Level Robustness Results ‣ VLAConf: Calibrated Task-Success Confidence for Vision-Language-Action Models") and[VI](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#A3.T6 "TABLE VI ‣ Appendix C Full Perturbation-Level Robustness Results ‣ VLAConf: Calibrated Task-Success Confidence for Vision-Language-Action Models").

Table[II](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#S5.T2 "TABLE II ‣ V-B Main Results ‣ V Experiments ‣ VLAConf: Calibrated Task-Success Confidence for Vision-Language-Action Models") shows that the main advantage of VLAConf persists under distribution shift after lightweight target-suite calibration. On OpenVLA-OFT, VLAConf and ConfidenceVLA have nearly identical success rates on both shifted suites, yet VLAConf substantially improves the proper scoring rules on both LIBERO-Pro and LIBERO-Plus. The ECE comparison is mixed—slightly worse on LIBERO-Pro but better on LIBERO-Plus—consistent with the standard-LIBERO results, where lower ECE alone does not necessarily indicate sharper per-rollout probabilities. The same representation-level head also remains applicable to the continuous-action \pi^{0.5} backbone, where action-token-based confidence methods are unavailable.

The full results in Appendix Tables[V](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#A3.T5 "TABLE V ‣ Appendix C Full Perturbation-Level Robustness Results ‣ VLAConf: Calibrated Task-Success Confidence for Vision-Language-Action Models") and[VI](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#A3.T6 "TABLE VI ‣ Appendix C Full Perturbation-Level Robustness Results ‣ VLAConf: Calibrated Task-Success Confidence for Vision-Language-Action Models") further show that the benefit of online execution evidence largely survives distribution shift. For OpenVLA-OFT, moving from pre-execution to online execution reduces both Brier score and NLL on LIBERO-Pro and LIBERO-Plus. The same pattern appears in Brier score for \pi^{0.5}, although its LIBERO-Pro NLL slightly increases. The perturbation-level breakdown also exposes residual failure modes rather than hiding them in the average: for \pi^{0.5}, semantic and lighting shifts produce high online NLL despite high success rates, suggesting that a small number of overconfident tail failures can still dominate likelihood under certain shifts. Overall, these shifted-suite results indicate that VLAConf remains informative beyond the original benchmark distribution while preserving the main benefits observed in the standard setting.

### V-D Real-World Robot Experiments

Finally, we evaluate whether the representation-level confidence signal remains useful beyond simulation by deploying VLAConf on a physical robot platform. As shown in Figure[4](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ V-B Main Results ‣ V Experiments ‣ VLAConf: Calibrated Task-Success Confidence for Vision-Language-Action Models"), the setup uses a Franka robot arm with a RealSense L515 camera, and demonstrations are collected through a Gello teleoperator. We consider three language-conditioned manipulation tasks: cup-on-table, duck-in-cooker, and towel-folding. These tasks cover rigid, articulated, and deformable manipulation, providing a deployment-facing complement to the benchmark studies above.

Table[III](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#S5.T3 "TABLE III ‣ V-C Robustness under Shifted LIBERO Suites ‣ V Experiments ‣ VLAConf: Calibrated Task-Success Confidence for Vision-Language-Action Models") summarizes task-success confidence over 151 physical rollouts. VLAConf remains effective in the real-world setting, with average ECE, Brier score, and NLL of 0.1041, 0.1595, and 0.4885 across the three tasks. The cup-on-table task reaches 48/50 successes and obtains the strongest proper-scoring-rule results, with Brier score 0.0527 and NLL 0.2361. Duck-in-cooker remains well calibrated in terms of ECE (0.0528), but exhibits higher Brier score and NLL. Towel-folding is the most challenging task in this set, achieving 24/48 successes and the largest error on all three calibration metrics. These results show that the confidence signal remains informative on physical rollouts while also reflecting the increased uncertainty of harder real-world manipulation tasks. The corresponding real-world calibration trends across task completion are provided in Appendix Figure[6](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29605#A1.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ A-B Real-World Calibration Across Task Completion ‣ Appendix A Additional Analyses ‣ VLAConf: Calibrated Task-Success Confidence for Vision-Language-Action Models").

## VI Conclusion

We propose VLAConf, a representation-level confidence framework for Vision-Language-Action models that derives calibrated task-success confidence from frozen internal features. Compared with existing confidence methods based on action-token probabilities or repeated sampling, VLAConf estimates confidence in a single forward pass and applies naturally to both discrete- and continuous-action backbones. Experiments on standard LIBERO suites and shifted LIBERO-Pro and LIBERO-Plus benchmarks demonstrate that VLAConf provides more informative task-success probabilities while remaining efficient, and that online evidence can further improve confidence quality during execution. Real-robot experiments further validate VLAConf.
